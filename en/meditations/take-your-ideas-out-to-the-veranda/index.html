Meditation 12

Take your ideas out to the veranda

November 17, 2026

“And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye? [...] Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

Matthew 7:3-5 · KJV

To think critically is not only to examine other people’s ideas. It is to take your own convictions out onto the veranda and hit them with a cricket bat, to see which ones still stand.

1. Your own ideas are the first ones to examine

It is easy to look for the flaws in what other people say. Real critical thinking begins when you apply the same test to what you yourself believe. Not to destroy yourself, but to find out what is still standing. If a conviction holds after you have struck it with all your strength, you can keep it with confidence. If not, it has done you a service, because you found out before you built on it. That is exactly the order Jesus asks for: first your own beam, then the mote in the other’s eye.

Scripture knows this gesture. The Bereans were called “more noble” because they searched the Scriptures even to check what an apostle told them (Acts 17:11). Paul asks: “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). The verb of testing comes first; holding on comes only after. And Proverbs says: “Be not wise in thine own eyes” (3:7).

2. To do that, you must see your prejudices

Prejudices do not feel like prejudices, but like “plain common sense.” You do not look at your glasses; you look through them. The beam in the eye is precisely what you cannot see: the eye does not see itself. It is the same with privileges: the one who has never gone without does not notice that others are playing on a sloping field. So examining your ideas requires an uncomfortable question: what prejudices lie behind what I believe, and what do I want to be true? The person who speaks to you honestly about that helps you more than the one who approves of you.

3. False dichotomies

Most disputes stay alive because we refuse nuance. A black-and-white world is more comfortable to live in: it offers clear camps, clear opponents, and spares us from holding two truths at the same time. That is how false dichotomies are born: “either/or,” when reality is often “both/and” or “it depends.” And once the dilemma is in place, a quiet temptation sets in: to defend my side, I start arguing any way I can, as long as it proves me right.

4. Two tennis courts

Here is the subtlest trap, and it is played out inside a single person. I defend a single point, but I support it with two entirely different systems of assumptions, without noticing.

Imagine a tennis match. For it to make sense, there must be a single court, with the same lines, the same net, the same rules: every shot is judged by shared criteria. Now imagine two separate courts, and me trying to win the same match by playing a little on one side, a little on the other. Each shot is splendid, well executed, but they never meet: what is “in” on the first court has no meaning on the second.

In life, that looks like this, for example: I invoke the authority of the Bible when it suits me, and reason alone when it does not. I judge my own mistakes by my intentions, and other people’s by their results. I demand scientific proof against my opponent’s idea, but I defend my own by personal experience. Each argument, taken on its own, looks solid. Together they do not hold, because they rest on values and criteria that do not speak to one another.

You recognize here the double standard Jesus denounces: “with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again” (Matthew 7:2). One measure for me and for the other, one court for every shot.

5. What to do, practically

  • Apply the bat first to your own conviction: what would have to be true for me to be wrong?
  • Write out your argument and list the assumptions each step rests on: which court am I playing on here? and there?
  • Check that you are using the same measure for yourself and for the other: would I accept this argument if it came from the opposite side?
  • Look for the nuance: where have I reduced a problem to two options, then chosen my criteria according to the result I wanted?

Questions for today

  • What is the conviction I have not put to the test in years?
  • Who has the right to tell me “you have something in your eye,” and when did they last do so?
  • On what point have I defended the same idea with two logics that contradict each other?

Prayer: “Lord, show me what I do not see, especially what I do not want to see. Make me humble enough to learn and courageous enough to remain with what still stands.”
